Thank you for clarifying. A flat wing with zero dihedral changes the picture significantly, and it explains a great deal about both the original instability and the current aggressive behaviour.

### The core characteristic: neutral roll stability
A wing with no dihedral has essentially **no inherent roll stability**. When a disturbance rolls the aircraft, it stays at that bank angle — it doesn’t try to return to level. This is because the mechanism that provides a self-righting moment (dihedral effect) relies on sideslip interacting with wing dihedral angle. Without dihedral, there’s no rolling moment due to sideslip (unless you have significant wing sweep, which acts like dihedral in some regimes, but we’ll assume a straight wing here).

- **Original large wings, no dihedral:** The aircraft was likely “unstable” in the sense that it would not recover from any roll disturbance; if disturbed, it would keep rolling until you corrected it. It may have felt twitchy because you were constantly fighting to keep the wings level. Large span also gave you high roll moment of inertia, so once it started rolling, it was harder to stop, making it feel uncontrollable.

- **Shorter wings, no dihedral:** You now have lower moment of inertia and less roll damping, so it reacts instantly to aileron input. However, because there’s still zero dihedral, the airframe has no desire whatsoever to level itself — it’s a pure “point and shoot” aeroplane. Every degree of bank you command, it holds until you command otherwise. That’s why it feels so “snappy” and why altitude loss is immediate if you overshoot the bank angle and don’t add elevator promptly.

### Why altitude loss is severe with no dihedral
When you roll into a bank, three things conspire to make you sink:

1. **No self-leveling tendency to moderate excessive bank.** You can very easily roll past the intended bank angle without realising it, especially with the now-hyperactive ailerons. A 45° bank becomes a 60° bank before you blink, and the vertical lift component collapses (at 60° bank, it’s only 50% of weight). That demands an immediate, substantial elevator pull that the human pilot rarely applies quickly enough.

2. **Adverse yaw goes unchecked by dihedral.** A flat wing with high aileron deflection produces strong adverse yaw. The drag from the downgoing aileron yaws the nose away from the turn. In a plane with dihedral, the resulting sideslip would create a rolling moment that opposes the ailerons (trying to level the wings), which naturally limits the bank rate. Without dihedral, the yaw simply causes a sideslip, which dramatically increases drag and makes the aircraft mushy; the bank continues unimpeded, and the aircraft sinks like a stone.

3. **The wing generates no extra lift from sideslip.** Dihedral wings convert a slight slip into a slight increase in lift on the forward wing, which can help maintain altitude in a gentle turn. Without dihedral, you lose that minor — but noticeable — assistance, so elevator compensation must be even more precise.

### Immediate setup adjustments (no structural changes)
Since the wing is flat, you must treat the roll axis with absolute electronic and mechanical finesse:

- **Drastic aileron throw reduction:** Mechanically reduce the aileron travel to perhaps 30–40% of what you currently have. You want the roll rate to feel deliberate, not like a switch. Use inner holes on the servo and outer holes on the aileron horn.
- **High expo (40–50%):** Softens the area around centre stick, preventing unintended micro-rolls into steep banks during turbulence or slight finger movements.
- **Aggressive aileron differential:** More up than down — try 2:1 or even 3:1 up/down ratio. This equalises drag between the two ailerons, drastically reducing adverse yaw. Many pilots of flat-wing aerobatic planes run extreme differential for precisely this reason.
- **Rudder mix is no longer optional:** Add about 25–35% rudder mixed to ailerons. This proactively coordinates the turn, eliminating the draggy sideslip and the associated sink. The improvement in altitude-holding can be dramatic.
- **Elevator pre-compensation mix:** A small down-elevator mix with throttle might help, but more useful is to train yourself or set a flight-mode condition where pulling aileron automatically adds a few percent of up-elevator if your radio supports it. Not strictly necessary, but helps bridge the pilot lag.

### The elegant structural fix: add a little dihedral
If you’re willing to modify the wing, adding just **2–3 degrees of dihedral** (a few mm of tip raise) will transform the aircraft’s manners without turning it into a docile trainer:

- It introduces a gentle self-righting moment that resists sharp rolling and makes the plane stop rolling when you release the stick — exactly what you’re missing.
- It will virtually eliminate the tendency to overshoot banks, so you can fly coordinated turns with much less workload.
- Roll response will still be crisp, just damped and linear instead of “all or nothing”.
- The altitude loss in turns will reduce markedly because the plane will naturally find a stable bank angle and you’ll have time to feed in elevator.

Even a small amount of dihedral goes a very long way. You can achieve it by building a new centre wing joiner with a slight bend, or by adding a tapered shim under the root rib if the construction allows.

### Summary for your flat-wing setup
The root cause of the aggressive roll and altitude loss is **zero dihedral combined with reduced roll damping**. You are flying an aerodynamically neutral roll axis that responds purely to your thumbs, with no aerodynamic “governor”. With the right radio setup (reduced throws, high expo, significant differential, and a proper aileron-rudder mix) you can fly it very precisely, but it will always demand active piloting. Adding a couple of degrees of dihedral is the single best thing you can do if you want it to feel easier and more forgiving in the turns.
